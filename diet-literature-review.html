3  Diet Composition: Literature Review

3.1 Overview

Juvenile Pacific salmon rearing in freshwater streams consume a mix of prey originating from aquatic, terrestrial, and in some rivers, marine sources. The relative contributions of these subsidies vary with season, river type, riparian vegetation, and fish species and size. Understanding how these proportions vary across space and time is central to interpreting the bioenergetics inputs used in Meyer et al. 2023, and to characterizing the prey base available to juvenile Chinook and Coho salmon in the Kenai River watershed.

This chapter reviews published literature on juvenile salmon diet composition, with a focus on:

  1. How prey subsidies from terrestrial, freshwater-aquatic, and marine sources are partitioned
  2. How those partitions vary across seasons, rivers, habitats, and fish attributes
  3. How authors have presented these data (metrics, visualization approaches)

Findings from this review inform the scope and presentation of the 2015-2016 diet composition summaries developed in subsequent chapters.

3.2 Key Papers

3.2.1 Rine et al. 2016: Susitna River, Alaska

Full citation: Rine, K.M., Wipfli, M.S., Schoen, E.R., Nightengale, T.L., and Stricker, C.A. 2016. Trophic pathways supporting juvenile Chinook and coho salmon in the glacial Susitna River, Alaska: patterns of freshwater, marine, and terrestrial food resource use across a seasonally dynamic habitat mosaic. Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 73(11): 1626–1641. https://doi.org/10.1139/cjfas-2015-0555

Author’s note: B. Meyer was a field technician for this study.

Relevance: High. Examines the same two species (Chinook, Coho) in a large south-central Alaska river system (Susitna), across multiple seasons and macrohabitat types. Integrates stable isotope analysis with stomach content data.

Methods summary:

  • Spatial coverage: 97 km segment of the Susitna River; multiple macrohabitat types (tributary mouths, side sloughs, upland sloughs, side channels, main channel)
  • Temporal coverage: multiple sampling events across the rearing season
  • Analytical methods: stable isotope analysis (δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N) combined with gut content analysis
  • Prey classification: freshwater-derived, marine-derived, and terrestrial-derived

Key findings:

  • Juvenile salmon were supported primarily by freshwater-derived resources and secondarily by marine and terrestrial sources (Rine et al. 2016).
  • The relative contribution of marine-derived prey was greatest in the fall within off-channel macrohabitats, whereas contributions of terrestrial invertebrate prey were generally greatest during midsummer, across all macrohabitats (Rine et al. 2016).

Presentation approach:

  • Prey subsidies presented as proportional contributions to diet by source (freshwater, marine, terrestrial)
  • Results disaggregated by macrohabitat type, season, and fish species
  • Both isotope mixing model outputs and stomach content proportions reported

Notes for our analysis: Our Kenai 2015-2016 data does not include stable isotope data, so we rely entirely on stomach content proportions. We lack the spatial complexity of multiple macrohabitat types within rivers, but we have multiple rivers and reaches within the Kenai watershed and multiple seasons. The three-way categorization (freshwater-aquatic, marine, terrestrial) used by Rine et al. maps reasonably well onto our prey categories.

3.2.2 Grunblatt et al. 2019: Kenai River Watershed, Alaska

Full citation: Grunblatt, J., B.E. Meyer, and M.S. Wipfli. 2019. Invertebrate prey contributions to juvenile Coho Salmon diet from riparian habitats along three Alaska streams: implications for environmental change. Journal of Freshwater Ecology 34(1):617-631. https://doi.org/10.1080/02705060.2019.1642243

Author’s note: B. Meyer is a co-author on this paper. Its data are the same as what is presented in Meyer et al. 2023.

Relevance: Very high. This study was conducted within the Kenai River watershed of southcentral Alaska and determined the proportion of total diet for stream-rearing juvenile Coho Salmon derived from terrestrial and aquatic winged adult invertebrates across three streams representative of habitats found throughout the region (Grunblatt et al. 2019).

Methods summary:

  • Species: Coho Salmon only
  • Sampling: seasonal stomach content sampling across two years
  • Prey classification: terrestrial invertebrates, aquatic winged adults, benthic aquatic invertebrates
  • Riparian habitat classification: grass/sedge, shrub, tree

Key findings:

  • The proportion of juvenile salmon diet represented by aquatic invertebrates was significantly different among study reaches, representing 62%, 55%, and 37% for Beaver Creek, Ptarmigan Creek, and the Russian River, respectively
  • Aquatic winged adult prey contributions were significantly lower in the grass/sedge study reach, and cross-ecosystem invertebrate prey represented a significantly higher proportion of diet in the tree study reach

Presentation approach:

  • Diet proportions presented by prey origin category (terrestrial, aquatic winged adult, benthic aquatic) across reaches and seasons
  • Statistical comparisons (ANOVA) among reaches for each prey category
  • Results disaggregated by study reach (river), not by fish species, age, or size class

3.2.3 Clitherow et al. 2024: Glacier Bay, Alaska

Full citation: Clitherow, L.R., S.H. Sønderland, F.M. Windsor, and A.M. Milner. 2024. Variation in the diet of juvenile salmonids across a gradient of stream development in Glacier Bay, Alaska. Pages 85-112 in A.J. Dumbrell and A.M. Milner, editors. Stream research in Glacier Bay, Alaska from 1977-2024: part 2. Advances in Ecological Research 71. https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.aecr.2024.10.005

Relevance: Moderate. Examines Coho salmon and Dolly Varden diets across streams of varying age since deglaciation, using gut content analysis combined with stable isotopes.

Key findings:

  • Juvenile salmonids, particularly coho salmon, in streams with higher channel sinuosity consumed a higher proportion of terrestrial prey
  • Patterns in diet reflected geomorphology, vegetational properties, and periodic disturbances in the watersheds

Presentation approach: Combines gut content analysis and stable isotope data to characterize dietary niche and prey source contributions across a stream development gradient.

Notes for our analysis: Reinforces the expectation that landscape-level factors (channel form, riparian vegetation) mediate terrestrial prey inputs, directly relevant to the landscape ecology framing of Meyer et al. 2023.

3.2.4 Duffy et al. 2010: Puget Sound Chinook Salmon

Full citation: Duffy, E.J., D.A. Beauchamp, R.M. Sweeting, R.J. Beamish, and J.S. Brennan. 2010. Ontogenetic diet shifts of juvenile Chinook salmon in nearshore and offshore habitats of Puget Sound. Transactions of the American Fisheries Society 139(3): 803-823. https://doi.org/10.1577/T08-244.1

Relevance: Moderate. Marine setting rather than freshwater, but provides a well-documented example of how diet composition data for Chinook salmon are analyzed and visualized across spatial and temporal groupings.

Key findings:

  • ANOVA was used to examine the effects of year, month, region, habitat, and size-class on proportions of prey groups in the diet of juvenile Chinook salmon.
  • Monthly diet composition was presented as wet-weight proportions of each prey category, disaggregated by region, with sample size and average fish length annotated on each bar.

Presentation approach: Stacked bar charts of wet-weight proportions, faceted by region (north, central, south), with time (month) on the x-axis. This is a clear example of the standard multi-panel format for presenting diet proportions across multiple grouping variables.

Notes for our analysis: Wet-weight proportions are the appropriate metric given our dry-to-wet mass conversion. The multi-panel stacked bar approach (faceted by a spatial or biological variable, with season on the x-axis) is a reasonable visualization format.

3.2.5 Kennedy et al. 2018: Eelgrass Habitat, Pacific Salmon

Full citation: Kennedy, L.A., F. Juanes, and R. El-Sabaawi. 2018. Eelgrass as valuable nearshore foraging habitat for juvenile Pacific salmon in the early marine period. Marine and Coastal Fisheries 10(2): 190-203. https://doi.org/10.1002/mcf2.10018

Relevance: Low-moderate. Marine context, but documents a common visualization approach.

Presentation approach: The proportion of each invertebrate group by count was assessed in juvenile salmon diets using a stacked bar graph. The proportion of total abundance of eelgrass-associated, planktonic, and terrestrial invertebrates in juvenile Chum and Chinook Salmon diets were calculated and presented alongside stable isotope mixing model results.

Notes for our analysis: Proportions by count (numerical) are an alternative to proportions by mass. Our raw data include both prey quantity (count) and prey dry mass. On energetic grounds, and for consistency with bioenergetics model inputs, mass-based proportions are the appropriate primary metric.

3.2.6 Syrjänen et al. 2011: Terrestrial Prey in Salmonid Diets

Full citation: Syrjänen, J., K. Korsu, P. Louhi, R. Paavola, and T. Muotka. 2011. Stream salmonids as opportunistic foragers: the importance of terrestrial invertebrates along a stream-size gradient. Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 68(12): 2146-2156. https://doi.org/10.1139/f2011-118

Key finding: In a quantitative literature review, the mean proportion of terrestrial prey in salmonid diets was 17%, being highest for the largest fish (≥15 cm).

Notes for our analysis: This provides a useful cross-study baseline. We have fish length data, so we can explore whether terrestrial prey proportion increases with fish size in our dataset.

3.3 Summary: Methods and Presentation Conventions

3.3.1 Metric for proportions

The most common metric is wet mass (or wet weight). Proportions by count are also used but are considered less informative for energetic modeling given the large size variation among prey items. Our dry-to-wet mass conversion produces the appropriate metric.

3.3.2 Prey categorization

Studies vary in granularity:

  • Coarse (3 groups): terrestrial, freshwater-aquatic, marine (used by Rine et al. 2016)
  • Intermediate (4–6 groups): e.g., separating aquatic larvae from aquatic adults, or distinguishing fish/eggs from invertebrates (common in gut-content-focused studies)
  • Fine (taxon-level): used in ordination or multivariate analyses, not typically in proportion summaries

Our prey categories (diet1: FishEggs, diet2: InvertAquatic_AqOrigin, diet3: InvertTerrestrial, diet4: InvertTerrestrial_AqOrigin, diet5: SalmonEggs) align with an intermediate scheme. We will present results at both the coarse level (terrestrial vs. aquatic vs. marine subsidy) and the intermediate level.

3.3.3 Analytical approaches

Most freshwater studies rely on gut content proportions alone. Stable isotope analysis corroborates or extends gut content findings but requires substantially more resources. Our study used gut content only, which is appropriate and typical for bioenergetics modeling contexts.

3.4 Implications for 2015-2016 Diet Composition Analysis

Based on this review, the analysis chapters that follow will:

  1. Present diet proportions as wet-mass-based proportions (proportion of total wet mass from each prey category per fish or group of fish)
  2. Adopt a two-level prey grouping: coarse (terrestrial / freshwater-aquatic / marine) and intermediate (the five bioenergetics diet categories)
  3. Disaggregate results by season × river × species × age where sample sizes allow
  4. Present primary visualizations as stacked bar charts, faceted by species and/or age, with season on the x-axis
  5. Compare observed proportions against the cross-study baseline (~17% terrestrial, with higher values for larger fish and in late summer)

Full citations for all works referenced in this chapter are collated in the References chapter.
