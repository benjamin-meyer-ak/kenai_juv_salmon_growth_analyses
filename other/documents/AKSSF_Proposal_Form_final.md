1.  [Synopsis]{.underline}

> This work will synthesize available freshwater temperature and
> biological data to characterize climate-related effects on juvenile
> salmon in the Cook Inlet, Copper River, and Prince William Sound
> regions. This project will compile available information and create
> novel datasets to assess temperature relationships with food quality
> and quantity and juvenile salmon body condition and growth. This work
> builds on prior thermal sensitivity modeling work in the region to
> assess how the changing freshwater thermal template could affect food
> availability and growth in the juvenile life stages of Pacific salmon.

2.  [Introduction]{.underline}

> Southcentral Alaska includes a confluence of the fastest growing
> population centers in the state, crucial salmon populations for
> subsistence, commercial, and personal use, and a wide range of
> documented climate and habitat threats to salmon-bearing watersheds.
> To maximize restoration, conservation, and monitoring efforts in this
> region, there is a need to understand how climate change will affect
> productivity of different types of watersheds in the future. Cook
> Inlet, Prince William Sound, and the Copper River have a mix of very
> warm, currently productive systems, and colder systems that are likely
> to experience an expansion of salmon habitat and productivity in the
> future as glaciers recede and freshwater temperatures increase
> [(Pitman et al., 2021)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hSRgNf).
> These changes to freshwater habitat will affect all freshwater life
> stages of all species of Pacific salmon.
>
> Climate change has the potential to affect salmon-bearing watersheds
> in multiple ways. First, warming water temperatures can influence
> freshwater life stages of Pacific salmon by causing pre-spawning
> mortality, changing emergence timing [(Adelfio et al., 2019; Campbell
> et al., 2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FfcwOg),
> influencing juvenile growth rates, and altering outmigration timing
> [(Wilson et al., 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FxtBfC).
> Second, climate can alter the freshwater habitat template of different
> types of watersheds. For example, climate-driven glacial recession
> could result in an increase in available salmon habitat in
> highly-glaciated watersheds, while an increase in extreme streamflow
> events [(Lader et al.,
> 2017)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tBJ2ZA) could cause
> scouring and mortality of incubating eggs and also impact juvenile
> rearing stages in other watersheds [(Gendaszek et al., 2018; Sloat et
> al., 2017)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YPsdiH). Finally,
> changes to temperature and flow regimes in freshwater habitat will
> affect freshwater prey availability and nutritional quality. For
> example, in large river systems, juvenile Chinook salmon relied on
> aquatic-riparian insects during flooding and zooplankton during
> drought [(Goertler et al.,
> 2018)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?d4bcGy).
>
> Watershed characteristics have a strong, but complex influence on
> multiple phases of Pacific salmon life cycles. For example, stream
> temperature differentially predicts population productivity, depending
> on the watershed [(Jones et al.,
> 2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?oB7T0R). As water
> temperatures warm, bioenergetics models predict that feeding rates and
> food quality become increasingly important for determining fish growth
> [(Railsback, 2022)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?q402PL), with
> these results confirmed by *in situ* experiments [(Lusardi et al.,
> 2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GVtwHG). Thus, to
> understand how climate change will affect salmon populations, it is
> critical to understand how prey resources respond to changing thermal
> regimes [(Meyer et al.,
> 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TPWAPI). Yet these
> relationships are poorly understood in Alaska. Recent research in the
> Kenai River watershed suggests that warming will not uniformly lead to
> an increase in juvenile salmon growth [(Meyer et al.,
> 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kaKJ1O). More information
> about prey availability during the freshwater rearing phase will be
> key to assessing climate impacts on salmon populations in Alaska.
>
> In Pacific salmon, juvenile growth rates may be especially sensitive
> to shifts in freshwater thermal regimes due to their longer freshwater
> residency times. In Alaska, these fish typically reside in streams for
> 1 year (Chinook Salmon) or 1-- 2 years (Coho Salmon) or lakes for 1-3
> years (Sockeye Salmon) before migrating to sea [(Quinn,
> 2005)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?c9ovkb). Freshwater growth
> rate is relevant because larger smolt size can influence age at marine
> entry, which in turn drives age structure and population stability
> [(Cline et al., 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?D6A0Vh),
> and growth is often positively correlated with marine survival
> [(Henderson & Cass, 1991; Ruggerone et al.,
> 2009)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?IADv6N). In addition, in
> sub-arctic regions of interior and western Alaska, warmer stream
> temperatures have been associated with increased juvenile growth of
> both Chinook Salmon [(Falke et al.,
> 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FTZn9J) and Coho Salmon
> [(Armstrong et al.,
> 2010)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?i687vp). However, it is
> unclear whether this pattern holds in more temperate regions with
> historically warmer thermal regimes, such as the Gulf of Alaska
> region, or how long it will persist before further warming leads to
> reduced growth [(Mauger et al., 2017; Shaftel et al.,
> 2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ICchJz). The effects of
> warming vary across geomorphically diverse watersheds, adding further
> complexity to how juvenile salmon growth responds to climate warming
> [(Lisi et al., 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kBwuqY).
>
> Juvenile salmon growth follows a hump-shaped curve, where growth
> increases with temperature up to a threshold. Past that thermal
> threshold, growth rates decrease as salmon become more thermally
> challenged. The availability of abundant and high quality food
> resources can increase the thermal optimum for growth of juvenile
> salmon up to a tolerance threshold that rarely exceeds 20C [(von Biela
> et al., 2020; Zillig et al.,
> 2021)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ScehvQ). In tandem with
> temperature, prey availability and nutritional quality determine
> juvenile growth rates and survival. These forces act in concert to
> drive salmon population dynamics. Aquatic insects comprise a large
> proportion of the diet of juvenile Pacific salmon [(Grunblatt et al.,
> 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?V8Xhzf). Aquatic insects
> themselves are also sensitive to changes in thermal regime and
> physical habitat, with shifts in community composition predicted under
> different climate change scenarios.
>
> Previous AKSSF-funded efforts in this region focused on mapping
> thermal sensitivities of salmon-bearing watersheds. The proposed
> project builds on that prior work to collate and synthesize
> information about freshwater prey resources, juvenile salmon body size
> and condition, and stream temperature with data on habitat threats and
> stream thermal sensitivities. That synthesis will identify critical
> watersheds in the Cook Inlet, Prince William Sound, and Copper River
> regions for future restoration, conservation, and monitoring efforts.

3.  [Location(s)]{.underline}

> The proposed work will include watersheds in the Cook Inlet and Prince
> William Sound regions, along with the Copper River watershed. These
> locations were selected because of potential data availability and
> their importance for numerous subsistence and personal-use fisheries
> near population centers in the state. Cook Inlet in particular
> contains multiple fisheries that support food security for a large
> number of Alaskans [(Harrison & Loring,
> 2016)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MQbzA2). Overall, the
> Chugach National Forest supports runs of all five Pacific salmon
> species, with particularly large runs of sockeye, pinks, and cohos
> [(Johnson et al., 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4ZjmWe).
> The Upper Copper River and Upper Susitna River Management Area
> supports three subsistence fisheries [(Somerville & Hansen,
> 2021)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GWgwRN). In both Cook Inlet
> and Copper River, Chinook salmon have experienced severe declines over
> the past decade, resulting in fishery closures and emergency orders
> and introducing the possibility of increased restrictions on
> subsistence fisheries in the future [(Somerville & Hansen,
> 2021)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5cMZHE).

4.  [Strategic Importance]{.underline}

> This project addresses the 2023 AKSSF objective under "Habitat
> Resiliency" to conduct climate impact assessments to guide the
> selection of future AKSSF projects. This project will result in a
> holistic webmapper highlighting both areas threatened by climate
> change and development pressure, as well as areas where adequate
> information about juvenile salmon body condition and freshwater prey
> availability are lacking to make informed management decisions. To our
> knowledge, this project would be the first regional effort to pull
> together data on freshwater prey availability for juvenile salmon in
> southcentral Alaska and would leverage and build on existing efforts
> to collate freshwater temperature data and juvenile body condition
> data across the state. The outcomes of this project could be used
> to 1) identify high-value habitats that are particularly crucial at
> present for juvenile salmon growth and outmigration; 2) highlight
> watersheds that have experienced changes in the quality and quantity
> of freshwater prey resources for juvenile salmon; 3) assess which
> habitats are likely to become more productive for juvenile salmon
> production in the future; and 4) inform data gaps, restoration and
> conservation priorities, and monitoring needs for future AKSSF-funded
> work.

5.  [Local Engagement]{.underline}

> This proposed work will build on connections from other funded work,
> including the Salmon Watershed Stewardship project hosted by ACCS,
> which focuses on building networks focused on understanding and
> monitoring salmon watersheds around Cook Inlet. This project includes
> place-based nonprofit organizations (Kenai Watershed Forum and Copper
> River Watershed Project) focused on watershed stewardship as funded
> project partners and will leverage their networks to identify and
> collaborate with interested parties in the region.

6.  [Habitat Conservation in Subsistence Areas]{.underline}

> Cook Inlet has five stocks of concern for Chinook salmon, including in
> McNeil River, Chuitna River, Theodore River, Alexander Creek, and East
> Susitna River. Cook Inlet, Prince William Sound and the Copper River
> all host important personal-use and subsistence fisheries. Cook Inlet
> has a federal subsistence fishery for residents of Hope, Cooper
> Landing, and Ninilchik, and state subsistence fisheries for Port
> Chatham, Windy Bay, Port Graham, Nanwalek, and Seldovia. In Prince
> William Sound, the Tatitlek and Chenega Areas both have state
> subsistence fisheries, and there are other subsistence fisheries in
> the general fishing districts around Prince WIlliam Sound, including
> the Copper River and Bering River. In Copper River, there are federal
> subsistence fisheries on the Upper and Lower Copper River and a state
> subsistence fishery in the Glennallen Subdistrict.
>
> The Cook Inlet region in particular holds some of the fastest-growing
> towns in the state, highlighting the need to understand the
> intersection of climate threats with development pressure. All three
> areas have watersheds that span a range of glacial cover, which will
> drastically alter watershed characteristics under climate change.
> These changes could influence the freshwater growth phase of juvenile
> salmon, both directly through temperature effects on growth rates and
> indirectly through changes in prey availability and nutritional
> quality.

VI. **Objectives**

- Objective 1: Create a freshwater macroinvertebrate database to assess
  data gaps and trends over space and time in freshwater prey sampling
  efforts.

- Objective 2: Build on an existing juvenile salmon body condition
  database to identify high value rearing habitats and fill and identify
  data gaps.

- Objective 3: Leverage newly launched and publicly available statewide
  freshwater temperature database (AKTEMP) to assess juvenile salmon
  body condition and prey availability across different thermal regimes
  and geomorphic settings.

- Objective 4: Synthesize biological data with development threat data
  in a public mapper to identify overlaps between uncovered data gaps
  and potential threats to freshwater habitat.

VII. **Methods**

> **[Objective 1: Freshwater macroinvertebrate database]{.underline}**
>
> This first objective will pull together data from multiple sources to
> create a freshwater macroinvertebrate database that can be used to
> assess the quantity and quality of freshwater prey availability for
> juvenile salmon across the region. This effort will be the first to
> create a harmonized database of freshwater prey availability in the
> region and will set the foundation for potential future statewide
> efforts to understand freshwater prey availability. Similar efforts
> have been made to collate information on marine food availability for
> Pacific salmon, and an enhanced understanding of importance of
> nutrition during the freshwater portion of their life cycle would aid
> in management efforts [(Graham et al.,
> 2020)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HTvyIz). Some watersheds in
> the study region have been sampled at multiple time points over the
> past several decades and can be used to analyze changes in freshwater
> prey availability and sampling efforts over that time period. This
> database could be leveraged for future statewide work and to build a
> database that could ingest and serve freshwater prey data statewide.
>
> [Task 1.1 Outreach to partners and possible data providers about data
> availability]{.underline}
>
> Multiple entities across the state collect macroinvertebrate data,
> either explicitly to assess freshwater prey availability or for other
> monitoring purposes (Figure 1). In the case of federal and state
> agencies, these data are made publicly available, either through
> web-based data portals or upon request. As a first step, the project
> team will create a list of possible data providers and reach out to
> request their participation in this work. The project team also has
> collected freshwater macroinvertebrate data across the region and will
> contribute these data to the database. Some members of the project
> team (e.g. CRWP) are interested in building capacity in
> macroinvertebrate monitoring, so as part of this task, the project
> team will also facilitate conversations about best practices and
> protocols for macroinvertebrate monitoring to support salmon
> conservation and restoration efforts.
>

> height="3.701330927384077in"}
>
> *Figure 1. Initial survey of existing temperature and
> macroinvertebrate sampling locations and metadata from ACCS, BLM, and
> ADEC sampling efforts.*

[Task 1.2 Establish and carry out workflow for data ingestion and
quality control]{.underline}

> A data workflow will be created to bring together disparate
> macroinvertebrate datasets from data contributors. This workflow will
> check for data completeness, including information on data collection
> methodology, sampling time period, and locality information. This
> process will be documented to ensure replicability and transparency
> for end-users and to inform potential future efforts in other regions.

[Task 1.3 Create a metadata webmapper]{.underline}

> Similar to prior efforts to build a statewide freshwater temperature
> database, this project will start with mapping metadata about who is
> collecting what types of macroinvertebrate data in which locations and
> during which time periods. Depending on the comparability of efforts,
> this project may also be able to collate the datasets into a
> harmonized database, but based on prior experience of the leadership
> team with creating macroinvertebrate databases in other settings, that
> could be a time-intensive task. At minimum, this task will result in a
> mapper that shares information about data collection of freshwater
> prey resources across the region, providing a foundation for future
> efforts to build a database that can ingest and serve data about
> juvenile salmon food resources.

[Task 1.4 Publish database on ACCS data catalog]{.underline}

> To maximize utility of the freshwater macroinvertebrate database, the
> data will be published on the ACCS data catalog. This entry will
> include the webmapper created in Task 1.3, as well as supporting
> documentation to inform users about the provenance of information in
> the database and how to use the database for their purposes.

**[Objective 2: Juvenile salmon body condition database]{.underline}**

> The statewide juvenile salmon body condition database currently
> contains 109,000 juvenile salmon length measurements and over 39,000
> weight measurements from across the state. These data were collated
> from 15 different research projects across the state. However, due to
> the statewide focus of this effort and the limited funds that
> supported its creation, an opportunity exists to expand on this effort
> to make this database even more useful in the future. By focusing on a
> specific region and conducting targeted outreach to potential data
> providers, the proposed work could be leveraged in the future in other
> regions across the state.

[Task 2.1 Evaluate data gaps in existing database]{.underline}

> While the existing database is extensive and covers the state, it
> still is lacking resolution and coverage in key areas for subsistence
> and personal use fisheries. Mapping locations of existing data will
> inform the next steps for targeted outreach to partners and potential
> data providers to build on this existing database. By also using
> coarse habitat filters (e.g. glaciated vs. non-glaciated watersheds),
> this project will also identify important habitat types that are
> currently underrepresented [(Bidlack et al.,
> 2014)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5dnZVe).
>
> [Task 2.2 Outreach to partners and potential data providers to
> supplement existing data]{.underline}
>
> The outcome of Task 2.1 will inform targeted outreach to potential
> data providers in areas where salmon-bearing watersheds are located
> but where juvenile salmon data are missing from the existing database.
> For example, the Susitna basin is currently well-represented in the
> database, while information about the Copper River is missing, but
> likely available from project partners. As part of this proposed work,
> the Copper River Watershed Project will convene their existing network
> of partners working on a coordinated temperature monitoring effort in
> order to identify existing data. They will also expand their juvenile
> salmon sampling and temperature monitoring in partnership with the
> Ahtna Intertribal Resource Commission into the headwaters of Tanada
> Creek and Copper Lake to add novel data to both the juvenile salmon
> database and the AKTEMP freshwater temperature database. A more
> focused and targeted effort to secure data for Southcentral Alaska
> could also be leveraged in future work in other regions across the
> state.

[Task 2.3 QA/QC final database and make spatially available on a
webmapper]{.underline}

> The current structure of the database is not spatially explicit,
> although the database contains locality data associated with body
> condition information. Turning this database into a webmapper will
> allow more users to interact with the data and to filter the database
> according to their geographic area of interest. The webmapper will
> include other filtering functionality so that users can, for example,
> select data from particular salmon species, sampling time periods, or
> data providers.
>
> [Task 2.4 Publish database to ACCS data catalog]{.underline}
>
> The database is currently planned to be archived in a public data
> repository like the Knowledge Network for Biocomplexity database as a
> collection of .csv files and R scripts. By including a linked record
> to that public repository and a functional webmapper on the ACCS data
> catalog, the database will be more visible and accessible to
> practitioners across the state. The data catalog entry will include
> information about data curation and associated metadata.
>
> **[Objective 3: Prey resource availability and juvenile salmon body
> condition across different thermal and geomorphic
> settings]{.underline}**
>
> AKTEMP is a publicly accessible database recently launched by ACCS in
> collaboration with Walker Environmental Research that takes in and
> distributes freshwater temperature data from across the state. The new
> launch of this database creates a unique opportunity to combine
> empirical data on water temperature, juvenile body condition, and prey
> availability to understand how thermal regimes and watershed
> geomorphic setting influence the freshwater juvenile life stage of
> Pacific salmon.
>
> [Task 3.1 Assess spatial and temporal overlaps between available
> temperature, prey availability, and juvenile salmon data]{.underline}
>
> While AKTEMP builds on an existing metadata mapper, AKOATS (Alaska
> Online Aquatic Temperature Site), the database is still being
> populated by data contributors (Figure 2), with continued and funded
> efforts planned to bring in more data. As part of AKSSF Project 51012
> to assess stream thermal sensitivities, ACCS has collaborated with
> partners in the same study area (Cook Inlet, Prince William Sound, and
> Copper River regions) to aggregate historical stream temperature
> datasets and perform quality control on summertime data. Data uploads
> to AKTEMP are performed by data collectors and ACCS will provide
> support to upload and review complete stream temperature time series
> for the study area so that these important datasets can be leveraged
> for this project and future research needs. AKTEMP can also store
> continuous and discrete lake temperature datasets and the project team
> will contact lake data collectors to facilitate importing these
> datasets into the database so that they can used to compare and
> contrast growth conditions for juvenile sockeye in the study area.
> Thus, this portion of the proposed work will focus on leveraging the
> AKTEMP database to identify watersheds where juvenile salmon body
> condition, prey resource, and freshwater temperature data have all
> been collected during comparable time periods.
>

> height="4.734375546806649in"}
>
> *Figure 2. Sites with temperature data uploaded to the AKTEMP
> database. Note the concentration of sites in the northern and eastern

>
> [Task 3.2 Analyze juvenile body condition and freshwater prey
> availability by thermal regime and geomorphic setting]{.underline}
>
> Using watersheds identified as having overlapping data availability
> during Task 3.1, this project will assess how juvenile salmon body
> condition and freshwater prey availability vary with freshwater
> temperature and watershed characteristics (e.g. watershed glaciation)
> across the study area. A large set of geomorphic attributes have
> already been created for a spatially balanced set of stream reaches
> across the proposed study area (Cook Inlet, Prince William Sound, and
> Copper River regions) as part of AKSSF Project 51012. These include
> stream slope, mean catchment elevation, mean catchment slope, mean
> watershed slope, percent of the watershed with a north aspect,
> watershed area, distance to the coast, and percent of the watershed
> covered by glaciers, lakes, or wetlands.
>
> ![](media/image1.png){width="5.813201006124235in"
> height="2.317674978127734in"}
>
> *Figure 3. The distribution of length data from the juvenile salmon
> database for September, with the Deshka River in 2019 highlighted as a
> separate distribution from the remainder of the available data.*
>
> As an example, preliminary analysis of the juvenile salmon database
> (Figure 3) suggests that warmer freshwater systems and warmer years,
> like the Deshka River in 2019, will result in populations of juvenile
> salmon that reach longer lengths and potentially out-migrate sooner
> than cooler systems. However, this earlier maturity and outmigration
> timing could result in phenological mismatches between smolts and
> their marine food resources, with consequences for returning adult
> spawners in the future [(Wilson et al.,
> 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MkXWYx). Better
> understanding of the juvenile freshwater portion of salmon life cycles
> across the region will help to identify these thermally sensitive
> habitats, as well as to predict which habitats might be more
> productive in the future.
>
> Additional work that could leverage this joined database include
> efforts to: 1) compare end of summer juvenile salmon size as a proxy
> for watershed productivity across streams of different geomorphic
> settings; 2) model juvenile salmon bioenergetics across the landscape
> including empirical data about freshwater prey availability; 3)
> predict freshwater prey quality and quantity under different climate
> change scenarios; 4) compare juvenile salmon productivity to adult
> return data across different thermal regimes; and 5) target future
> sampling and monitoring in areas with little temporal and spatial
> overlap in temperature, prey, and juvenile salmon data.

**[Objective 4: Data synthesis]{.underline}**

> This final objective will synthesize the datasets compiled in the
> prior three objectives with additional data about watershed thermal
> sensitivities and current and future habitat threats to inform future
> AKSSF project selection.
>
> [Task 4.1 Create webmapper combining AKTEMP, juvenile salmon data, and
> freshwater prey data with thermal sensitivities from prior AKSSF work
> and current and future habitat threats.]{.underline}
>
> Leveraging prior AKSSF-funded work on thermal sensitivities of salmon
> habitats (Figure 4), this proposed work will create a synthesis
> webmapper that brings together freshwater temperature data, juvenile
> salmon data, and freshwater prey data with thermal sensitivity data
> and current and proposed development threats to salmon habitat.
>

>
> [Task 4.2 Assess overlaps between data gaps and sensitive watersheds
> to inform future AKSSF projects]{.underline}
>
> The combined webmapper will be used to rank watersheds that are both
> sensitive to climate change and development threats and that are
> currently lacking data availability to monitor these changes. Based on
> prior work on thermal sensitivities in the region, all three study
> areas (Cook Inlet, Copper River, and Prince William Sound) have
> watersheds that span a range of thermal sensitivities (Figure 5). It
> is anticipated that some of the most thermally sensitive watersheds

> available data. However, the less thermally sensitive areas, such as
> those found at high elevations or in highly glacierized watersheds
> (Figure 4), might represent habitat that could become more productive
> for juvenile salmon in the future, which would motivate expanding
> restoration, conservation, and monitoring efforts in those watersheds.
> As an outcome of this task, this project will create a watershed
> prioritization based on value for juvenile rearing under current and
> future climatic conditions.

VIII. **Benefits**

> The outcomes of the proposed work will identify watersheds that are
> both threatened by climate change and current and proposed development
> and that lack sufficient data to assess those potential impacts. Those
> watersheds could then be targeted for enhanced monitoring,
> restoration, and conservation efforts by future AKSSF projects. This
> work will also highlight watersheds that are currently productive for
> juvenile salmon or that might experience an increase in productivity
> in the future as water temperatures rise. Those watersheds could then
> be prioritized for AKSSF-funded conservation and restoration efforts.
